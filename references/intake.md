# Intake and the Clarifying Round

The council can only build a plan as specific as the brief it receives. This file covers the twelve slots that brief contains, how to fill them without interrogating the user, and what to do with what remains unknown.

The governing idea: **a question is worth asking only if the plan changes depending on the answer.** Everything else is either inferable, or belongs in the Assumption Ledger where the user can correct it later at a glance.

---

## The twelve brief slots

Fill each from the user's message and the workspace scan before asking anything. Tag each one **GIVEN** (stated by the user or found in a named file), **INFERRED** (reasoned from what you have — say from what), or **UNKNOWN**.

### 1. The business and the offer
What is sold, at what price, at what margin, and what does the buyer get?

- Good: "Study-abroad counselling and application handling. Free consultation, service fee at offer stage, commission from partner universities on enrolment."
- Thin: "We help students." A plan cannot be built against a service that has no price and no commercial mechanism, because nobody can tell what a lead is worth.

### 2. The objective
The business outcome, with a number and a date. **Blocker — cannot be assumed.**

- Good: "60 enrolments for the September 2026 intake, decided by March."
- Not an objective: "grow awareness," "more leads," "launch the newsletter." The first two have no magnitude, the third is a task.
- If the user genuinely does not have a number, do not invent one — help them derive it: what would make this worth doing, and what does the business need?

### 3. The baseline
What the numbers are *now*. The most-skipped slot in marketing planning and the one that most often makes the objective absurd.

- Good: "42 enrolments last intake, ~900 leads, ~30% show up to consultation."
- Why it matters: a target of 60 against a baseline of 42 is a plan. A target of 60 against a baseline of 4 is a different plan entirely, and no channel mix works for both.
- If unknown, say so loudly in the ledger. A plan with an objective and no baseline cannot tell success from noise.

### 4. The audience
Who, in what situation, at what trigger moment, currently doing what instead. **Blocker — cannot be assumed.**

Demographics alone do not qualify. What the council needs is a person in a moment: what just happened in their life that makes this relevant now, and what they would do if this brand did not exist.

### 5. The decision unit
Everyone involved in the purchase: the one who wants it, the one who pays, the one who can veto, the one whose opinion is asked.

Frequently the whole plan turns on this. Marketing to the student when the parent signs the cheque, or to the manager when procurement decides, produces campaigns that perform well on every metric except revenue.

### 6. The competitive set and why people currently choose them
Who else gets this money, and what the honest reason is that they win.

The useful version is uncomfortable. "They are cheaper," "they have an office near the university," "the buyer's cousin used them" are real answers. "They market more aggressively" usually is not.

### 7. Proof assets
What the brand can actually point to: results, numbers, testimonials, case studies, credentials, partnerships, track record, founder story.

This slot silently determines what the Brand Strategist and Creative Director can build. A brand with fifteen years of results plans differently from one with none, and a plan that claims proof the business does not have is worse than useless.

### 8. Budget
Money committed, over what period, and whether it is fixed or arguable. **Near-blocker — a range is acceptable, silence is not.**

A genuinely zero-budget plan is a valid answer and must be stated as such, because it changes every section. What cannot happen is six seats each assuming a different budget: one writes a paid-media plan, another an organic-only plan, and the two never meet.

### 9. Team and capacity
Who does the work, how many hours a week, with what skills and what tools.

Marketing Ops cannot write a calendar without this, and the capacity gate cannot be enforced without it. "Me, about 8 hours a week, plus a designer on retainer" is a completely different plan from "a team of five."

### 10. Timeline and seasonality
The deadline, and the shape of the year. Intake cycles, seasons, fiscal timing, hiring cycles, exam calendars, religious and holiday periods.

Seasonality is where generic plans expose themselves fastest. A campaign scheduled through a period when the audience is unreachable is a plan that wasted its best month.

### 11. What is already running, and what has been tried
Existing channels, current performance, and — most valuable — what has already failed and how.

A plan that confidently proposes the thing that flopped last year destroys the user's trust in the entire document. Ask for the graveyard.

### 12. Constraints and non-negotiables
What is off the table: brand rules, legal limits, channels that cannot be used, claims that cannot be made, things leadership has already ruled out, partner obligations.

Constraints are a gift to a planning council. Getting them late means throwing away good work.

## The clarifying round

### Step 1 — never ask what you already have

Fill all twelve slots first. Asking about something the user just told you, or that is written in the company profile sitting in the workspace, is the fastest way to make the whole session feel mechanical.

### Step 2 — rank the gaps by plan-changing power

For each UNKNOWN slot, ask yourself: **if the answer flipped to its opposite, how much of the plan would be rewritten?**

- **High** — budget, objective, audience, capacity, decision unit. Flip any of these and the plan is unrecognizable. Ask.
- **Medium** — baseline, competitive set, proof assets, seasonality. Flip these and sections change. Ask if there is room in the four.
- **Low** — tooling preferences, brand colours, naming, format opinions. The council can build without them and the user can correct them in ten seconds when they read the plan. Never spend a question here.

### Step 3 — ask at most four, in one round, with defaults

Use the host's structured question tool when available, otherwise ask in ordinary chat. Respect the tool's question and option limits. Every question gets 2-4 concrete options and a recommended default listed first with "(Recommended)" in its label, so the user can accept the whole round in a single pass and still get a good plan.

The recommendation is doing real work here. A user who does not know their own answer will pick the recommended option, which means your recommendation must be genuinely defensible rather than a placeholder. Make each option a real position with a real consequence, not a vague band.

**Good question design:**

> **Budget** — What can this campaign actually spend over the next 90 days?
> - Under $2,000 total (Recommended if unsure) — organic-led plan, one paid channel used only for retargeting
> - $2,000-10,000 — one paid acquisition channel funded properly plus organic
> - $10,000-50,000 — multi-channel with real testing budget
> - Effort only, no cash — the plan is built entirely from time

Each option implies a visibly different plan. That is what makes it worth a question.

**Bad question design:**

> What are your marketing goals?

Open-ended, unanswerable in one click, and it produces a sentence the council still has to interpret.

### Step 4 — one round, two maximum

A second round happens only when an answer opened a genuinely new fork — the user said "we sell through agents, not direct," and the whole channel structure now depends on something you had no reason to ask about before. It is not for tidying up loose ends.

Never a third. At some point the honest move is to build the plan on stated assumptions and let the user correct a concrete document, which is far easier for them than answering abstract questions about their own business.

### Step 5 — the three blockers

**Objective, audience, and offer cannot be assumed.**

These three are load-bearing for all six seats simultaneously. Without them, each seat invents its own version, all six sections silently describe different businesses, and the conflict round surfaces contradictions that are artefacts of the missing brief rather than real tensions in the plan. The output looks like a council product and is noise.

If any of the three is still missing after two rounds, stop. Say exactly what you need and why the plan cannot be built without it. Returning a short, honest gap list beats returning a confident plan built on three inventions.

Everything else — including budget — can proceed on a stated assumption.

## The Assumption Ledger

Every slot still UNKNOWN after the clarifying round becomes a ledger entry. The ledger travels with the brief to all six seats and is printed in the final plan.

Each entry has three parts:

```
ASSUMPTION:  What we are treating as true.
BASIS:       Why this assumption rather than another — a benchmark, an
             inference from the workspace, or "arbitrary, needs confirming."
IF WRONG:    What in the plan changes, and how much. Be specific about which
             section breaks.
```

Worked example:

```
ASSUMPTION:  Consultation-to-enrolment conversion is around 20-25%.
BASIS:       Category benchmark for advisory-led education services. No internal
             baseline was available.
IF WRONG:    Everything downstream of the funnel math. At 10% the plan needs
             roughly double the lead volume, which the stated budget cannot buy —
             the objective would have to drop to ~35 enrolments or the budget
             roughly double. Confirm this number before spending anything.
```

**Why the ledger matters more than it looks.** A plan built on unmarked assumptions is indistinguishable from a plan built on facts, right up until it fails, at which point nobody can work out which link broke. The ledger converts invisible risk into a short checklist the user can verify in an afternoon — and it is usually the single most useful page in the document.

Sort the ledger by blast radius, largest first. The top entry is the thing to go and confirm before any money moves, and the plan should say so.
